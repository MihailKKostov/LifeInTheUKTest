@section The UK today
The UK is located in the **north west of Europe**. The longest distance on the mainland is from **John O'Groats** (north coast of Scotland) to **Land's End** (south-west England): about **870 miles (1,400 km)**.

Post-war immigration means **nearly 10%** of the population has a parent or grandparent born outside the UK.

## Capital cities
| UK | **London**
| Scotland | **Edinburgh**
| Wales | **Cardiff**
| Northern Ireland | **Belfast**

Other major cities: in **England** – Birmingham, Liverpool, Leeds, Sheffield, Bristol, Manchester, Bradford, Newcastle upon Tyne, Plymouth, Southampton, Norwich; in **Wales** – **Swansea** and **Newport**; in **Scotland** – **Glasgow, Dundee** and **Aberdeen**.

## Money
The currency is the **pound sterling (£)**; there are **100 pence** in a pound.
- Coins: **1p, 2p, 5p, 10p, 20p, 50p, £1 and £2**
- Notes: **£5, £10, £20 and £50**

**Scotland and Northern Ireland** have their own banknotes. They are **valid everywhere in the UK**, but shops **do not have to accept them**.

## Languages
English has many accents and dialects. In **Wales**, many people speak **Welsh**, which is taught in schools and universities. In **Scotland**, **Gaelic** is spoken in parts of the **Highlands and Islands**. In **Northern Ireland**, some people speak **Irish Gaelic**.

Growth has been faster recently, due to **migration and longer life expectancy**. The population is unevenly spread: **England about 84%**, **Scotland just over 8%**, **Wales about 5%** and **Northern Ireland less than 3%**.

People are **living longer** than ever – there are a **record number aged 85 and over**, which affects the cost of pensions and health care.

## An equal and diverse society
The population is **ethnically diverse**, especially in large cities like London. The most common ethnic description chosen in surveys is **white**; other significant groups are **Asian, black and mixed** descent.

Men and women have **equal rights** to work, own property, marry and divorce, and married parents are **equally responsible** for their children. Women make up **about half the workforce**, girls leave school with **better qualifications** than boys on average, and **more women than men study at university**.

@section Religion and patron saints
The UK is historically a **Christian** country. In the **2009 Citizenship Survey**:
| Christian | **70%**
| No religion | **21%**
| Muslim | **4%**
| Hindu | **2%**
| Sikh | **1%**
| Jewish or Buddhist | Less than **0.5%** each
| Other religion | **2%**

Everyone has the legal right to **choose their religion, or no religion**.

## The Church of England
In England there is a **constitutional link between Church and state**. The **Church of England** (called the **Anglican Church** in other countries and the **Episcopal Church** in Scotland and the USA) is a **Protestant** Church, existing since the **Reformation in the 1530s**.

- The **monarch is the head** of the Church of England.
- The **spiritual leader** is the **Archbishop of Canterbury**.
- The monarch can select the Archbishop and senior officials, but usually the choice is made by **the Prime Minister and a Church committee**.
- Several Church of England **bishops sit in the House of Lords**.

## Other Churches
The national Church of Scotland is the **Church of Scotland**, a **Presbyterian** Church governed by **ministers and elders**. Its chairperson, the **Moderator**, is appointed for **one year** only.

There is **no established Church in Wales or Northern Ireland**. Other Protestant groups include **Baptists, Methodists, Presbyterians and Quakers**. The biggest other Christian denomination is **Roman Catholic**.

@section Festivals and traditions
## Christian festivals
- **Christmas Day, 25 December** – the birth of Jesus Christ; a public holiday. Many go to church on **Christmas Eve (24 December)**. Traditional food: **roast turkey, Christmas pudding and mince pies**. Children believe **Father Christmas (Santa Claus)** brings presents.
- **Boxing Day, 26 December** – a **public holiday**.
- **Easter** (March or April) – marks the death of Jesus on **Good Friday** and his rising on **Easter Sunday**. **Good Friday and Easter Monday** are public holidays. **Easter eggs** symbolise new life.
- **Lent** – the **40 days before Easter**, when people often give something up. It begins on **Ash Wednesday**. The day before, **Shrove Tuesday**, is **Pancake Day**.

## Other religious festivals
- **Diwali** – October or November, **five days**, the **Festival of Lights**. Celebrated by **Hindus and Sikhs**; it celebrates **the victory of good over evil**. Famous celebration in **Leicester**.
- **Hannukah** – November or December, **eight days**. **Jewish**; a candle is lit each day on a **menorah**.
- **Eid al-Fitr** – celebrates **the end of Ramadan**, the month Muslims fast.
- **Eid ul Adha** – remembers that **Ibrahim** was willing to sacrifice his son. Animals sacrificed in Britain must be killed in a **slaughterhouse**.
- **Vaisakhi** – **14 April**, a **Sikh** festival celebrating the founding of the **Khalsa**.

## Other festivals and traditions
| 1 January | **New Year** – public holiday. In Scotland 31 December is **Hogmanay** and **2 January** is also a holiday.
| 14 February | **Valentine's Day** – lovers exchange cards and gifts.
| 1 April | **April Fool's Day** – jokes **until midday**.
| March/April | **Mothering Sunday** – the Sunday **three weeks before Easter**.
| June | **Father's Day** – the **third Sunday in June**.
| 31 October | **Halloween** – roots in a **pagan** festival marking the start of winter; "trick or treat" and pumpkin lanterns.
| 5 November | **Bonfire Night** – fireworks remembering the failed plot of **1605**, when **Guy Fawkes** and Catholics tried to blow up Parliament and the Protestant king.
| 11 November | **Remembrance Day** – remembers those who died fighting for the UK. People wear **poppies**; a **two-minute silence at 11.00 am**; wreaths laid at the **Cenotaph** in Whitehall.

## Bank holidays
Public holidays with **no religious significance**, when banks and many businesses close: **early May, late May/early June, and August**. In Northern Ireland the **Battle of the Boyne** anniversary in **July** is also a holiday.

@section Sport
Many famous sports began in Britain, including **cricket, football, lawn tennis, golf and rugby**. Major stadiums include **Wembley Stadium** (London) and the **Millennium Stadium** (Cardiff).

## The Olympics and Paralympics
The UK has hosted the **Olympic Games three times: 1908, 1948 and 2012**. The main 2012 site was in **Stratford, East London**, and Britain finished **third in the medal table**.

The **Paralympics** began with the work of **Dr Sir Ludwig Guttmann**, a German refugee, at **Stoke Mandeville** hospital in **Buckinghamshire**, helping people with spinal injuries.

## Popular sports
- **Football** is **the UK's most popular sport**. Each UK country has its own league and national team. The **English Premier League** has a huge international audience. **England's only international tournament victory** was the **1966 World Cup**, hosted in the UK.
- **Cricket** originated in England; games can last up to **five days** and still end in a draw. The most famous competition is **the Ashes** – **England v Australia**. Phrases like "it's just not cricket" and "batting on a sticky wicket" come from it.
- **Rugby** originated in England in the **early 19th century**. There are two types: **union** and **league**. The most famous union competition is the **Six Nations**: **England, Ireland, Scotland, Wales, France and Italy**. The **Super League** is the best-known rugby league club competition. Northern Ireland plays with the **Irish Republic**.
- **Horse racing** dates back to **Roman times** and has links with royalty: **Royal Ascot** (five days, **Berkshire**), the **Grand National** at **Aintree near Liverpool**, and the **Scottish Grand National** at **Ayr**. The **National Horseracing Museum** is in **Newmarket, Suffolk**.
- **Golf** dates back to **15th-century Scotland**. **St Andrews** is known as **the home of golf**. **The Open Championship** is the only "Major" held outside the USA.
- **Tennis** evolved in England in the **late 19th century**; the first club was founded in **Leamington Spa in 1872**. **Wimbledon**, at the All England Lawn Tennis and Croquet Club, is **the oldest tennis tournament in the world** and **the only Grand Slam played on grass**.
- **Sailing**: **Sir Francis Chichester** was the first to sail **single-handed around the world (1966/67)**; two years later **Sir Robin Knox-Johnston** did it **without stopping**. The most famous sailing event is at **Cowes on the Isle of Wight**. The **Oxford v Cambridge** boat race is rowed on **the Thames**.
- **Motor sport**: car racing in the UK started in **1902**. A **Formula 1 Grand Prix** is held every year; recent British champions include **Damon Hill, Lewis Hamilton and Jensen Button**.
- **Skiing**: there are **five ski centres in Scotland** and **Europe's longest dry ski slope near Edinburgh**.

@section Music and theatre
## Classical music
**The Proms** is an **eight-week summer** season of orchestral music, including at the **Royal Albert Hall**, organised by the **BBC since 1927**. The **Last Night of the Proms** is the best-known concert.

> Henry Purcell (1659–95) :: Organist at **Westminster Abbey**; developed a distinctly British style of church music and opera.

> George Frederick Handel (1695–1759) :: **German-born**, became a British citizen in **1727**. Wrote **Water Music** for **George I**, **Music for the Royal Fireworks** for **George II**, and the oratorio **Messiah**, often sung at Easter.

> Gustav Holst (1874–1934) :: Wrote **The Planets**. **Jupiter** became the tune for the hymn **I Vow to Thee My Country**.

> Sir Edward Elgar (1857–1934) :: Born in **Worcester**. Best known for the **Pomp and Circumstance Marches**; March No 1 (**Land of Hope and Glory**) is played at the **Last Night of the Proms**.

> Ralph Vaughan Williams (1872–1958) :: Wrote for orchestras and choirs; strongly influenced by **English folk music**.

> Sir William Walton (1902–83) :: Wrote **coronation marches** for **George VI and Elizabeth II**; best known for **Façade** (became a ballet) and **Belshazzar's Feast**.

> Benjamin Britten (1913–76) :: Best known for the operas **Peter Grimes** and **Billy Budd**, and **A Young Person's Guide to the Orchestra** (based on Purcell). Founded the **Aldeburgh festival** in **Suffolk**.

## Popular music
Since the 1960s British pop has been hugely influential – **The Beatles** and **The Rolling Stones**, the **Punk** movement of the **late 1970s**, and **boy and girl bands** in the **1990s**.
- Big venues: **Wembley Stadium**, **The O2** in **Greenwich**, and the **SECC** in **Glasgow**.
- Summer festivals: **Glastonbury**, the **Isle of Wight Festival** and the **V Festival**.
- The **National Eisteddfod of Wales**: an annual festival of music, dance and art, largely **in Welsh**, with competitions for Welsh poetry.
- The **Mercury Music Prize** (each **September**) is for the best album from the **UK and Ireland**. The **Brit Awards** give awards such as best British group.

## Theatre
London's **West End** is known as **"Theatreland"**. **The Mousetrap**, a murder mystery by **Agatha Christie**, has run there since **1952** – **the longest initial run of any show in history**.

- **Gilbert and Sullivan** wrote 19th-century **comic operas**: **HMS Pinafore, The Pirates of Penzance** and **The Mikado**.
- **Andrew Lloyd Webber** wrote the music for **Jesus Christ Superstar** and **Evita** (with **Tim Rice**), and **Cats** and **The Phantom of the Opera**.
- **Pantomime** is a Christmas tradition, based on fairy stories. The **Dame** is a woman played by a man, and there is often a **pantomime horse or cow**.
- The **Edinburgh Festival** takes place every **summer**; its biggest part is the **Edinburgh Festival Fringe**, mainly theatre and comedy, often experimental.
- The **Laurence Olivier Awards** are held annually in **London**, named after the actor best known for Shakespeare roles.

## Art
In the Middle Ages most art was **religious**. In the 16th and 17th centuries many painters in Britain came from abroad, like **Hans Holbein** and **Sir Anthony Van Dyck**. British **portrait and landscape** painters became famous from the 18th century.

Galleries: the **National Gallery, Tate Britain and Tate Modern** (London), the **National Museum** in **Cardiff**, and the **National Gallery of Scotland** in **Edinburgh**.

The **Turner Prize** for **contemporary art** was set up in **1984**, named after Joseph Turner. **Four works are shortlisted** and shown at **Tate Britain**. Winners include **Damien Hirst** and **Richard Wright**.

## Architecture
- **Middle Ages**: great cathedrals at **Durham, Lincoln, Canterbury and Salisbury**. The **White Tower** in the Tower of London is a **Norman keep** built for **William the Conqueror**.
- Great country houses such as **Hardwick Hall** in **Derbyshire**.
- **17th century**: **Inigo Jones** designed the **Queen's House at Greenwich** and the **Banqueting House** in Whitehall. **Sir Christopher Wren** designed the new **St Paul's Cathedral**.
- **18th century**: simpler designs. Scottish architect **Robert Adam** designed **Dumfries House** inside and out; his ideas influenced the **Royal Crescent in Bath**.
- **19th century**: **gothic** style returned – the **Houses of Parliament**, **St Pancras Station** and town halls in **Manchester and Sheffield**.
- **20th century**: **Sir Edwin Lutyens** designed **New Delhi** and many war memorials, including the **Cenotaph** in Whitehall.
- **Modern** architects: **Sir Norman Foster, Lord (Richard) Rogers** and **Dame Zaha Hadid**.

## Gardens and design
**Lancelot "Capability" Brown** (18th century) designed natural-looking grounds for country houses. **Gertrude Jekyll** designed colourful gardens, often with **Lutyens**. The annual **Chelsea Flower Show** showcases garden design.

Designers: **Thomas Chippendale** (18th-century **furniture**), **Clarice Cliff** (**Art Deco ceramics**), **Sir Terence Conran** (**interior** design). Fashion: **Mary Quant, Alexander McQueen, Vivienne Westwood**.

## Gardens, shopping and food
Many people garden; some rent an **allotment** to grow fruit and vegetables. Famous gardens: **Kew Gardens, Sissinghurst and Hidcote** (England), **Crathes Castle and Inveraray Castle** (Scotland), **Bodnant Garden** (Wales) and **Mount Stewart** (Northern Ireland).

## Film
Films were first shown publicly in the UK in **1896**. **Sir Charles (Charlie) Chaplin** became famous in silent films for his **tramp** character. In the 1930s, **Sir Alexander Korda** and **Sir Alfred Hitchcock** were leading directors. In the war, films like **In Which We Serve** boosted morale. Later, **Sir David Lean** and **Ridley Scott** found international success.

- The **1950s and 1960s** were a high point for comedies: **Passport to Pimlico, The Ladykillers** and the **Carry On** films.
- The two highest-grossing film franchises – **Harry Potter and James Bond** – were produced in the UK.
- **Ealing Studios** claims to be **the oldest continuously working film studio** in the world.
- **Nick Park** has won **four Oscars**, three for **Wallace and Gromit**.
- Recent British Oscar winners: **Colin Firth, Sir Anthony Hopkins, Dame Judi Dench, Kate Winslet, Tilda Swinton**.
- The **BAFTAs** are the British equivalent of the Oscars.

Famous films: **The 39 Steps** (1935, Hitchcock), **Brief Encounter** (1945, Lean), **The Third Man** (1949), **Lawrence of Arabia** (1962, Lean), **Chariots of Fire** (1981), **Four Weddings and a Funeral** (1994), **Touching the Void** (2003).

## Comedy
Medieval kings had **jesters**. Political cartoons grew in the 18th century, and the satirical magazine **Punch** first appeared in the **1840s** (today **Private Eye** continues the tradition). Music hall stars like **Morecambe and Wise** moved to TV. TV satire included **That Was The Week That Was** (1960s) and **Spitting Image** (1980s–90s). **Monty Python's Flying Circus** began in **1969**.

## TV and radio
Popular soaps include **Coronation Street** and **EastEnders**. There is a **Welsh-language** channel in Wales and a **Gaelic** channel in Scotland.

Everyone who watches TV needs a **TV licence** – one per home (people with **separate tenancies** in a shared house each need one). People **over 75** can apply for a **free licence** and **blind people get 50% off**. Watching without a licence can bring a fine of **up to £1,000**.

The licence fee pays for the **BBC**, the **largest broadcaster in the world** and **the only wholly state-funded media organisation independent of government**. Other channels are funded by **advertising and subscriptions**.

## Pubs, betting and pets
- You must be **18** to buy alcohol in a pub or club. At **16** you can drink **wine or beer with a meal** in a hotel or restaurant if with someone over 18.
- Pubs usually open at **11.00 am** (**12 noon on Sundays**); the **licensee** decides closing hours. **Pool and darts** are traditional pub games.
- You must be **18** to enter betting shops or gambling clubs, and to play the **National Lottery**.
- It is illegal to treat a pet **cruelly or neglect** it. Dogs in public must wear a **collar with the owner's name and address**, and owners must **clean up** after them. **Vets** provide animal care; charities help those who can't pay.

@section Places of interest
There are **15 national parks** in England, Wales and Scotland. The **National Trust** (founded in **1895 by three volunteers**) and the **National Trust for Scotland** are **charities** that preserve buildings, coastline and countryside; more than **61,000 volunteers** help.

| Big Ben | Nickname of the **great bell** of the clock at the **Houses of Parliament**. The clock is over **150 years** old. The tower was renamed **Elizabeth Tower** for the **Diamond Jubilee in 2012**.
| Eden Project | In **Cornwall**. Giant greenhouses called **biomes** with plants from around the world; also a charity.
| Edinburgh Castle | Dominates Edinburgh's skyline; looked after by **Historic Scotland**.
| Giant's Causeway | **North-east coast of Northern Ireland**. Columns of **volcanic lava** about **50 million years** old.
| Loch Lomond and the Trossachs | National park in the **west of Scotland**. **Loch Lomond** is the **largest expanse of fresh water in mainland Britain**.
| London Eye | Ferris wheel on the **south bank of the Thames**, **135 metres (443 ft)**, built for the **millennium**.
| Snowdonia | National park in **North Wales**. **Snowdon** is the **highest mountain in Wales**.
| Tower of London | First built by **William the Conqueror**. Tours by **Yeoman Warders (Beefeaters)**; home of the **Crown Jewels**.
| Lake District | **England's largest national park**. Biggest lake: **Windermere**. **Wastwater** was voted **Britain's favourite view** (2007).
