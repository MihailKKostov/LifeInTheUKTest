@title A long and illustrious history
@subtitle From the Stone Age to the present day
@intro The biggest chapter and the source of many questions. Told here in order, from the first farmers to today. Focus on **dates**, **who did what**, **battles**, **Acts of Parliament** and how **Parliament and the right to vote** developed.

@section Early Britain
## The Stone Age
The first people in Britain were **hunter-gatherers**. For much of the Stone Age, Britain was joined to the continent by a land bridge. It became an island, separated by the Channel, about **10,000 years ago**.

The **first farmers arrived about 6,000 years ago**, probably from **south-east Europe**. They built houses, tombs and monuments – including **Stonehenge** in **Wiltshire**, probably a gathering place for seasonal ceremonies. **Skara Brae on Orkney** is the **best-preserved prehistoric village in northern Europe**.

## The Bronze and Iron Ages
Around **4,000 years ago** people learned to make **bronze**. They lived in **roundhouses** and buried their dead in tombs called **round barrows**, and were skilled metalworkers in bronze and gold.

In the **Iron Age** people made tools and weapons from iron and built defended sites called **hill forts** – an impressive one is **Maiden Castle in Dorset**. They spoke languages from the **Celtic** family (related languages survive in Wales, Scotland and Ireland) and made **the first coins minted in Britain**, some inscribed with the names of Iron Age kings. This marks **the beginning of British history**.

## The Romans (AD 43 – AD 410)
**Julius Caesar** invaded in **55 BC** but failed. In **AD 43 Emperor Claudius** led a successful invasion. **Boudicca**, queen of the **Iceni** in eastern England, fought the Romans – her **statue stands on Westminster Bridge** near Parliament.

Parts of Scotland were never conquered. **Emperor Hadrian** built **Hadrian's Wall** to keep out the **Picts** (ancestors of the Scots). Forts such as **Housesteads** and **Vindolanda** can still be seen; it is a **UNESCO World Heritage Site**.

The Romans stayed for **400 years**, building roads and public buildings, creating a structure of law and introducing new plants and animals. The **first Christian communities** appeared in the **3rd and 4th centuries AD**.

## The Anglo-Saxons
The Roman army left in **AD 410**. Britain was invaded by the **Jutes, Angles and Saxons** from northern Europe, whose languages are **the basis of modern English**. By about **AD 600** Anglo-Saxon kingdoms were established, mainly in England; Wales and Scotland stayed free of them.

A king was buried with treasure and armour in a ship at **Sutton Hoo in Suffolk**.

Missionaries brought Christianity. From Ireland: **St Patrick** (patron saint of Ireland) and **St Columba**, who founded a monastery on **Iona**. From Rome: **St Augustine**, who became **the first Archbishop of Canterbury**.

## The Vikings
The Vikings came from **Denmark and Norway**, first raiding in **AD 789**. The Anglo-Saxon kingdoms united under **King Alfred the Great**, who **defeated the Vikings**. Many Vikings settled in the east and north of England in the **Danelaw** – place names like **Grimsby** and **Scunthorpe** come from Viking languages.

There was a short period of Danish kings, the first being **Cnut (Canute)**. In the north, the Viking threat led people to unite under **Kenneth MacAlpin**, and the name **Scotland** began to be used.

## The Norman Conquest (1066)
In **1066 William, Duke of Normandy** defeated **Harold**, the Saxon king, at the **Battle of Hastings**. William became **William the Conqueror**. The battle is shown in the **Bayeux Tapestry**, which can still be seen **in France**.

It was **the last successful foreign invasion of England**. Norman French influenced the English language. The Normans conquered Wales at first (the Welsh won land back) but **did not invade Scotland**.

William ordered a survey of every town and village, who owned the land and what animals they had: the **Domesday Book**, which still exists today.

@section The Middle Ages
The period from 1066 to about **1485** is the **Middle Ages** (medieval period) – a time of almost constant war.

## Wars at home and abroad
- **Wales**: in **1284 Edward I** introduced the **Statute of Rhuddlan**, annexing Wales to the English Crown. Huge castles such as **Conwy** and **Caernarvon** were built. The last Welsh rebellions were defeated by the **mid-15th century**.
- **Scotland**: in **1314** the Scots, led by **Robert the Bruce**, defeated the English at the **Battle of Bannockburn**. Scotland remained unconquered.
- **Ireland**: by **1200** the English ruled an area around **Dublin** known as **the Pale**.
- Abroad: knights fought in the **Crusades** for the Holy Land, and England fought France in the **Hundred Years War** – which actually lasted **116 years**. At the **Battle of Agincourt (1415)**, **Henry V**'s outnumbered army defeated the French. The English left France in the **1450s**.

## Feudalism and the Black Death
The Normans used **feudalism**: the king gave land to lords in return for help in war. Most peasants were **serfs** who worked their lord's land and could not move away. In the north of Scotland and Ireland, land was owned by **clans**.

In **1348** the **Black Death** (probably plague) killed **one third of the population of England**, and a similar share in Scotland and Wales. Afterwards there were labour shortages, peasants demanded higher wages, a new class of large landowners appeared (**the gentry**), and people moved to towns, where a strong **middle class** developed.

## Magna Carta and Parliament
In **1215 King John** was forced by his noblemen to agree to the **Magna Carta** ("Great Charter"). It established that **even the king was subject to the law**, protected the rights of the nobility and restricted the king's power to collect taxes or change laws.

Parliament grew from the king's council. Two Houses formed: the **House of Lords** (nobility, great landowners, bishops) and the **House of Commons** (knights and wealthy townspeople, elected by a small part of the population). Scotland's Parliament had **three Houses called Estates**: the lords, the commons and the clergy.

The principle that **judges are independent of government** began. English judges developed **common law** by **precedent** (following previous decisions); in Scotland laws were **codified** (written down).

## Language and culture
Norman French and Anglo-Saxon combined to become **English**. By **1400** official documents were in English, and it was the preferred language of the royal court and Parliament.

**Geoffrey Chaucer** wrote **The Canterbury Tales**, poems about pilgrims travelling to Canterbury. It was one of the first books printed by **William Caxton**, **the first person in England to print books** with a printing press. In Scotland, **John Barbour** wrote **The Bruce** about Bannockburn.

Castles (e.g. **Windsor** and **Edinburgh**, still in use) and great cathedrals (e.g. **Lincoln**) were built; **York Minster** is famous for its stained glass. **English wool** was a major export.

## The Wars of the Roses
In **1455** a civil war began between the **House of Lancaster** (**red rose**) and the **House of York** (**white rose**). It ended at the **Battle of Bosworth Field in 1485**: **Richard III** (York) was killed and **Henry Tudor** (Lancaster) became **King Henry VII**. He married **Elizabeth of York**, uniting the families. The **Tudor rose** is a **red rose with a white rose inside**.

@section The Tudors
**Henry VII** strengthened central government, reduced the power of the nobles and built up the monarchy's finances. His son **Henry VIII** is famous for **breaking away from the Church of Rome** and **marrying six times**.

## The six wives of Henry VIII
| 1 | **Catherine of Aragon** – Spanish princess; mother of **Mary**. Henry divorced her.
| 2 | **Anne Boleyn** – English; mother of **Elizabeth**. **Executed** at the Tower of London.
| 3 | **Jane Seymour** – gave Henry his son **Edward**; died shortly after the birth.
| 4 | **Anne of Cleves** – German princess, married for political reasons; **divorced**.
| 5 | **Catherine Howard** – cousin of Anne Boleyn; **executed**.
| 6 | **Catherine Parr** – a widow; she **survived** Henry.

! Memory aid: divorced, beheaded, died, divorced, beheaded, survived.

When the **Pope refused** Henry a divorce, he established the **Church of England**, in which **the king, not the Pope**, appointed bishops. This happened alongside the **Reformation** across Europe – a Protestant movement against the Pope's authority. Protestants read the Bible in their own language and did not pray to saints. In Ireland, attempts to impose Protestantism led to rebellion.

Under Henry VIII, Wales was formally united with England by the **Act for the Government of Wales**.

## Edward VI, Mary and Elizabeth I
**Edward VI** was strongly Protestant; the **Book of Common Prayer** was written in his reign. He died at **15**. His half-sister **Mary**, a devout Catholic, persecuted Protestants and became known as **"Bloody Mary"**.

**Elizabeth I** (daughter of Henry VIII and Anne Boleyn) was Protestant and re-established the Church of England, finding a balance between Catholics and extreme Protestants. She became one of the most popular monarchs, especially after **1588**, when the English **defeated the Spanish Armada**, sent to conquer England and restore Catholicism.

## Scotland and Mary, Queen of Scots
In **1560** the Scottish Parliament abolished the Pope's authority and a Protestant **Church of Scotland** was set up – but, unlike in England, **not a state Church**.

**Mary Stuart (Mary, Queen of Scots)** was Catholic and became queen at a week old. Suspected of involvement in her husband's murder, she fled to England and gave her throne to her Protestant son **James VI**. Elizabeth I, her cousin, kept her prisoner for **20 years** and she was eventually **executed** for plotting against Elizabeth.

## Exploration, poetry and drama
The Elizabethan period was a time of growing **patriotism**. **Sir Francis Drake**, a commander against the Armada, sailed the **Golden Hind**, one of the first ships to **sail around the world**. English settlers first began to **colonise the east coast of America**.

> William Shakespeare (1564–1616) :: Born in **Stratford-upon-Avon**. Playwright and actor; famous plays include **A Midsummer Night's Dream, Hamlet, Macbeth** and **Romeo and Juliet**. He was one of the first to portray **ordinary English people** and invented many words still used today. The **Globe Theatre** in London is a modern copy of the theatres where his plays were first performed.

Famous lines and where they come from:
- "Once more unto the breach" – **Henry V**
- "To be or not to be" – **Hamlet**
- "A rose by any other name" – **Romeo and Juliet**
- "All the world's a stage" – **As You Like It**
- "The darling buds of May" – **Sonnet 18**

@section The Stuarts, civil war and revolution
## James VI and I
Elizabeth I never married. When she died in **1603** her cousin **James VI of Scotland** became **James I of England, Wales and Ireland** – but Scotland remained a **separate country**. His reign produced the **King James Version** (Authorised Version) of the **Bible** in English.

In Ireland, the English encouraged Scottish and English Protestants to settle in **Ulster**, taking land from Catholics. These settlements were called **plantations** and had serious long-term consequences.

## The road to civil war
**James I** and his son **Charles I** believed in the **Divine Right of Kings** – that the king was appointed by God and should not need Parliament's approval. Charles I tried to rule **without Parliament for 11 years**.

Charles tried to impose a new Prayer Book on the **Presbyterian Church in Scotland**, causing a Scottish army to form. In **1640** he recalled Parliament for money; many MPs were **Puritans** (strict Protestants) and refused. Charles entered the House of Commons to arrest five leaders, who escaped – **no monarch has entered the Commons since**.

**Civil war began in 1642**: the king's supporters were the **Cavaliers**; Parliament's supporters were the **Roundheads**. The king's army was defeated at **Marston Moor** and **Naseby**. Charles I was **executed in 1649**.

## The Commonwealth: England's only republic
England became a republic called **the Commonwealth**. **Oliver Cromwell** crushed the revolt in Ireland so violently that he remains controversial there. The Scots crowned **Charles II**, but Cromwell defeated him at **Dunbar** and **Worcester**; Charles escaped, famously **hiding in an oak tree**.

Cromwell ruled as **Lord Protector** until his death in **1658**. His son Richard could not control the army, and after **11 years** as a republic people wanted a king again.

## The Restoration (1660)
In **1660 Charles II** returned from exile in the Netherlands. The Church of England was again the official Church.
- **1665**: a major outbreak of **plague** in London.
- **1666**: the **Great Fire of London** destroyed much of the city, including **St Paul's Cathedral**, rebuilt by **Sir Christopher Wren**. **Samuel Pepys** described these events in his **diary**.
- **1679**: the **Habeas Corpus Act** – Latin for "you must present the person in court" – guaranteed **no one could be held prisoner unlawfully**.
- The **Royal Society**, **the oldest surviving scientific society in the world**, was formed. Early members included **Edmund Halley** (Halley's Comet) and **Isaac Newton**.

> Isaac Newton (1643–1727) :: Born in **Lincolnshire**, studied at **Cambridge**. His **Principia Mathematica** showed how **gravity** applied to the whole universe. He also discovered that **white light is made up of the colours of the rainbow**.

## The Glorious Revolution (1688)
Charles II's brother **James II** (James VII of Scotland) was a **Roman Catholic**. When his son was born, a Catholic succession seemed likely. In **1688** Protestants invited **William of Orange**, the Protestant ruler of the Netherlands and husband of James's daughter **Mary**, to take the throne. There was **no fighting** in England, so it was called the **Glorious Revolution**. William and Mary ruled jointly.

James tried to win back the throne through Ireland but was defeated at the **Battle of the Boyne in 1690** (still celebrated by some in Northern Ireland). A Scottish rising was defeated at **Killiecrankie**; the **MacDonalds of Glencoe** were massacred for being late to swear loyalty. James's supporters became known as **Jacobites**.

- **Plantations** – Protestant settlers in **Ulster**
- **Divine Right of Kings** – James I and Charles I
- **1642** Civil War: **Cavaliers** (king) v **Roundheads** (Parliament); king beaten at **Marston Moor** and **Naseby**
- **1649** Charles I executed; republic = **the Commonwealth**; **Cromwell** = **Lord Protector**

@section Union, Enlightenment and industry
## Constitutional monarchy
At William and Mary's coronation a Declaration of Rights was read. The **Bill of Rights (1689)** confirmed **the rights of Parliament and the limits of the king's power**: the monarch must be **Protestant**, could not raise taxes without Parliament, and a new Parliament had to be elected at least every **three years** (later seven, **now five**).

Monarchs now needed ministers with a majority in Parliament. The two main groups were the **Whigs** and the **Tories** (the Conservatives are still called Tories) – **the beginning of party politics**. From **1695** newspapers no longer needed a government licence: the start of a **free press**.

This was **not yet democracy**: only men who **owned property** could vote and **no women** could. Constituencies controlled by one wealthy family were **"pocket boroughs"**; those with hardly any voters were **"rotten boroughs"**.

## Newcomers
The **first Jews since the Middle Ages** settled in London in **1656**. Between **1680 and 1720**, **Huguenots** – French **Protestants** persecuted for their religion – came as refugees; many were skilled scientists, bankers and weavers.

## Great Britain is born (1707)
Queen Anne had no surviving children. The **Act of Union** (the **Treaty of Union** in Scotland) of **1707** created **the Kingdom of Great Britain**. Scotland kept its own **legal and education systems** and **Presbyterian Church**.

## The first Prime Minister
When Anne died in **1714**, Parliament chose **George I**, a **German** and her nearest Protestant relative. He did not speak good English, so relied on his ministers. The most important became known as **Prime Minister** – the first was **Sir Robert Walpole (1721–1742)**.

## Bonnie Prince Charlie
In **1745** **Charles Edward Stuart (Bonnie Prince Charlie)**, grandson of James II, landed in Scotland supported by Highland clansmen. He was defeated at the **Battle of Culloden in 1746**. The clans lost power, and in the **Highland Clearances** landlords destroyed small farms (**crofts**) to make way for sheep and cattle; many Scots emigrated to North America.

> Robert Burns (1759–96) :: Known as **"The Bard"**. Scottish poet who wrote in **Scots**, English with Scottish words, and standard English. Best known for **Auld Lang Syne**, sung at New Year (**Hogmanay** in Scotland).

## The Enlightenment
In the **18th century** new ideas about politics, philosophy and science developed. Many great thinkers were **Scottish**: **Adam Smith** (economics), **David Hume** (human nature) and **James Watt** (steam power). A key principle: everyone has the right to their own political and religious beliefs.

## The Industrial Revolution
In the **18th and 19th centuries** Britain became **the first country to industrialise on a large scale**, thanks to machinery and **steam power**. The **Bessemer process** for mass-producing **steel** boosted shipbuilding and railways. **Canals** linked factories to towns and ports. Working conditions were very poor, and **children** worked too.

> Richard Arkwright (1732–92) :: Trained as a **barber**. Improved the **carding machine** for preparing fibres, developed **horse-driven spinning mills** and later used **steam power**. Remembered for running his factories **efficiently and profitably**.

> Sake Dean Mahomet (1759–1851) :: From **Bengal**, India. In **1810** he opened the **Hindoostane Coffee House** in London – **the first curry house in Britain**. He and his wife also introduced **"shampooing"** (Indian head massage).

Britain expanded overseas: **Captain James Cook** mapped the coast of **Australia**, Britain gained **Canada**, and the **East India Company** took control of large parts of **India**.

## The slave trade
British prosperity was partly built on the **slave trade**. Slavery was illegal within Britain itself, but slaves from **West Africa** were shipped in terrible conditions to work on plantations in America and the Caribbean.

The **Quakers** set up the **first formal anti-slavery groups**. **William Wilberforce**, an evangelical Christian MP, helped turn public opinion. In **1807** trading slaves in British ships or ports became illegal, and in **1833** the **Emancipation Act** abolished **slavery throughout the British Empire**. Afterwards **2 million Indian and Chinese workers** replaced the freed slaves.

## America and France
The American colonies objected to British taxes: **"no taxation without representation"**. In **1776** **13 colonies declared independence**, and Britain recognised it in **1783**.

Britain fought France and Napoleon. At the **Battle of Trafalgar (1805)**, **Admiral Nelson** defeated the French and Spanish fleets but was killed. **Nelson's Column** is in Trafalgar Square, and his ship **HMS Victory** is in **Portsmouth**. In **1815** the **Duke of Wellington** – "the **Iron Duke**", later Prime Minister – defeated Napoleon at the **Battle of Waterloo**.

## The Union Flag
In **1801** Ireland was united with Great Britain (Act of Union **1800**), creating the **United Kingdom of Great Britain and Ireland**. The **Union Flag** (Union Jack) combines three crosses:
- **St George** (England) – **red cross on white**
- **St Andrew** (Scotland) – **diagonal white cross on blue**
- **St Patrick** (Ireland) – **diagonal red cross on white**

The **Welsh dragon** is not on it because Wales was already united with England when the first Union Flag was created in **1606**.

@section The Victorian age and the Empire
**Queen Victoria** became queen in **1837 aged 18** and reigned until **1901** – almost **64 years**. Britain's power grew abroad and the **middle classes** became more important at home.

## The British Empire
The Empire grew to cover **India, Australia and large parts of Africa** – **the largest empire the world has ever seen**, with more than **400 million people**. Between **1853 and 1913** as many as **13 million** British citizens emigrated. Between **1870 and 1914** around **120,000 Russian and Polish Jews** came to Britain to escape persecution.

## Trade, industry and transport
Britain promoted **free trade**: the **Corn Laws**, which stopped cheap grain being imported, were **repealed in 1846**. In **1847** the working day for **women and children** was limited to **10 hours**.

**George and Robert Stephenson** pioneered the **railway engine**. Britain produced **more than half the world's iron, coal and cotton cloth**. In **1851** the **Great Exhibition** opened in **Hyde Park** in the **Crystal Palace**, a building of steel and glass.

> Isambard Kingdom Brunel (1806–59) :: Engineer from **Portsmouth** who built tunnels, bridges, railways and ships. He built the **Great Western Railway**, the first major railway in Britain, from **Paddington** to the south-west, West Midlands and Wales.

## The Crimean War (1853–56)
Britain fought with **Turkey and France against Russia**. It was **the first war extensively covered by the media**. Queen Victoria introduced the **Victoria Cross** medal for valour.

> Florence Nightingale (1820–1910) :: Born in **Italy** to English parents. Nursed soldiers in **Turkey** during the Crimean War and cut the death rate. In **1860** she founded the **Nightingale Training School** at **St Thomas' Hospital**, London. Regarded as **the founder of modern nursing**.

## Ireland
In the mid-19th century the **potato crop failed** and Ireland suffered a **famine**: **a million died** and **a million and a half emigrated**. The nationalist movement grew: the **Fenians** wanted full independence, while **Charles Stuart Parnell** wanted **Home Rule** (Ireland's own parliament within the UK).

## The right to vote
- **1832 Reform Act**: greatly increased voters, **abolished pocket and rotten boroughs** and gave seats to towns and cities. Voting was still based on **property**.
- The **Chartists** campaigned for votes for the working classes.
- **1867 Reform Act**: more urban seats and a lower property requirement – but most men and **no women** could vote.

Until **1870** a married woman's earnings and property belonged to her husband; Acts in **1870 and 1882** let wives keep them. Women campaigning for the vote became known as **suffragettes**.

> Emmeline Pankhurst (1858–1928) :: Born in **Manchester**. Set up the **Women's Franchise League (1889)** and helped found the **Women's Social and Political Union (WSPU)** in **1903**, the first "suffragettes". They chained themselves to railings, smashed windows and went on hunger strike. In **1918** women **over 30** got the vote; in **1928**, shortly before her death, women got the vote at **21, the same as men**.

## The future of the Empire
The **Boer War (1899–1902)** in South Africa against Dutch settlers (**Boers**) made people question the Empire. Over the 20th century there was a mostly orderly transition from **Empire to Commonwealth**.

> Rudyard Kipling (1865–1936) :: Born in **India**. Believed the British Empire was **a force for good**. Won the **Nobel Prize in Literature (1907)**. Wrote the **Just So Stories**, **The Jungle Book** and the poem **If**, often voted among the UK's favourite poems.

" If you can keep your head when all about you
" Are losing theirs and blaming it on you…
"— If, Rudyard Kipling

@section The First World War and after
## A time of optimism and reform
In the early 20th century Britain was a global superpower. Reforms included **financial help for the unemployed, old-age pensions and free school meals**, and **MPs were paid a salary for the first time**.

## The First World War (1914–18)
On **28 June 1914 Archduke Franz Ferdinand** of Austria was assassinated, triggering the war. Britain was among the **Allied Powers** (with France, Russia, Japan, Belgium, Serbia and later Greece, Italy, Romania and the USA). They fought the **Central Powers** – mainly **Germany, Austria-Hungary, the Ottoman Empire** and later **Bulgaria**.

The whole Empire took part: more than **a million Indians** fought for Britain (around **40,000** were killed). Britain suffered over **2 million casualties**. The attack on the **Somme in July 1916** caused about **60,000 British casualties on the first day**.

The war ended at **11.00 am on 11 November 1918** with victory for Britain and its allies.

## The partition of Ireland
In **1913** the government promised **Home Rule** for Ireland, opposed by Protestants in the north. The war postponed it. In **1916** Irish nationalists rose up in Dublin: the **Easter Rising**. Its leaders were executed and a guerrilla war followed.

A peace treaty was signed in **1921**, and in **1922 Ireland became two countries**: the six mainly Protestant counties in the north stayed in the UK as **Northern Ireland**; the rest became the **Irish Free State**, which became a **republic in 1949**. The later conflict over this split is known as **"the Troubles"**.

## Between the wars
In **1929** the world entered the **Great Depression**. Heavy industries like **shipbuilding** suffered mass unemployment, while new **car and aviation** industries grew. **Car ownership doubled from 1 to 2 million** between 1930 and 1939. Writers **Graham Greene** and **Evelyn Waugh** and economist **John Maynard Keynes** were prominent. The **BBC** began radio broadcasts in **1922** and the **world's first regular television service in 1936**.

@section The Second World War
**Adolf Hitler** came to power in Germany in **1933**. When he **invaded Poland in 1939**, **Britain and France declared war**.

The **Axis powers** were **Germany, Italy and Japan**. The main Allies were the **UK, France, Poland, Australia, New Zealand, Canada and the Union of South Africa**.

## 1940: Britain stands alone
As Germany advanced through France in **1940**, **Winston Churchill** became Prime Minister.

- **Dunkirk**: civilian volunteers in small boats helped the Navy rescue **more than 300,000 men** from the beaches – the origin of **"the Dunkirk spirit"**.
- From **June 1940 until June 1941** Britain and the Empire **stood almost alone** against Nazi Germany.
- **The Battle of Britain** (summer **1940**): the RAF won the battle for the skies with **Spitfires and Hurricanes**, designed and built in Britain.
- **The Blitz**: German bombing of cities at night. **Coventry** was almost totally destroyed, as was much of London's **East End** – hence **"the Blitz spirit"**.

> Winston Churchill (1874–1965) :: Son of a politician; a soldier and journalist before becoming a **Conservative MP in 1900**. PM from **May 1940**; he refused to surrender. He **lost the 1945 election** but returned as PM in **1951**. Given a **state funeral** in **1965**, and voted **the greatest Briton of all time** in **2002**.

" I have nothing to offer but blood, toil, tears and sweat
"— First speech as PM, 1940
" We shall fight on the beaches … we shall never surrender
"— After Dunkirk, 1940
" Never in the field of human conflict was so much owed by so many to so few
"— During the Battle of Britain, 1940

## Victory
Japan defeated the British in **Singapore** and occupied **Burma**. The **USA entered the war** after Japan bombed **Pearl Harbor in December 1941**. Hitler's invasion of the **Soviet Union** (1941) failed with huge losses.

On **6 June 1944**, allied forces landed in **Normandy** – **D-Day**. Germany was defeated in **May 1945**. The war against Japan ended in **August 1945** after the USA dropped atom bombs on **Hiroshima and Nagasaki**. Scientists led by **Ernest Rutherford** (Manchester, then Cambridge) were first to **split the atom** and took part in the **Manhattan Project**.

> Alexander Fleming (1881–1955) :: Born in **Scotland**. Discovered **penicillin in 1928** while researching flu. **Howard Florey and Ernst Chain** developed it into a usable drug, mass-produced by the 1940s. **Nobel Prize in Medicine (1945)**.

@section Britain since 1945
## The welfare state
In **1945** the British people elected a **Labour** government under **Clement Attlee**, who introduced the welfare state outlined in the **Beveridge Report**. In **1948** **Aneurin (Nye) Bevan**, Minister for Health, led the creation of the **National Health Service (NHS)**, free at the point of use. Railways, coal mines and gas, water and electricity were **nationalised**.

In **1947** independence was granted to **nine countries**, including **India, Pakistan and Ceylon (Sri Lanka)**. The UK developed an **atomic bomb** and joined **NATO**.

> Clement Attlee (1883–1967) :: Born in London; a barrister who did social work in East London. **Churchill's Deputy PM** in the wartime coalition, then **PM 1945–51**. Nationalised major industries, created the **NHS** and led Labour for **20 years**.

> William Beveridge (1879–1963) :: Liberal economist and social reformer. His **1942 report** – the **Beveridge Report** – called for fighting five **"Giant Evils": Want, Disease, Ignorance, Squalor and Idleness**. It was the basis of the **modern welfare state**.

> R A Butler (1902–82) :: Conservative MP responsible for education from 1941. The **Education Act 1944** ("**The Butler Act**") introduced **free secondary education** in England and Wales, and the split between **primary and secondary** schools.

> Dylan Thomas (1914–53) :: **Welsh** poet. Wrote the radio play **Under Milk Wood** and the poem **Do Not Go Gentle into That Good Night**. Died aged 39 in New York; memorials in his birthplace **Swansea**.

There was a **Conservative** government from **1951 to 1964**. PM **Harold Macmillan** made the **"wind of change"** speech about decolonisation.

## Migration after the war
Labour shortages led the government to invite workers from **Ireland and Europe**, and in **1948** from the **West Indies**. In the 1950s buses recruited in the West Indies, and textile and engineering firms recruited in **India and Pakistan**. Migration fell in the **late 1960s** when new laws restricted it. In the early 1970s Britain admitted **28,000 people of Indian origin** forced to leave **Uganda**.

## The Swinging Sixties
The **1960s** brought growth in fashion, cinema and pop music (**The Beatles** and **The Rolling Stones**). Laws on **divorce and abortion** were liberalised, and new laws gave women **equal pay** and banned sex discrimination at work.

## The 1970s
The post-war boom ended. Strikes caused conflict between the **trade unions** and government. In Northern Ireland, the Parliament was **suspended in 1972** and direct rule from London began; some **3,000** people died in the violence after **1969**.

> Mary Peters (1939–) :: Born in Manchester, moved to **Northern Ireland**. Won **Olympic gold in the pentathlon in 1972**, then promoted sport and tourism in NI. Made a **Dame in 2000**.

## Europe
The **EEC** was formed in **1957** by **West Germany, France, Belgium, Italy, Luxembourg and the Netherlands**. The UK **joined in 1973**.

## Thatcher and the Conservatives (1979–1997)
> Margaret Thatcher (1925–2013) :: A grocer's daughter from **Grantham, Lincolnshire**; trained as a chemist and lawyer. **Britain's first woman Prime Minister (1979–1990)** and the **longest-serving PM of the 20th century**. **Privatised** nationalised industries, limited trade union power and worked closely with US President **Ronald Reagan**.

In **1982 Argentina invaded the Falkland Islands**; a British naval taskforce recovered them. Traditional industries like shipbuilding and coal mining declined. **John Major** followed Thatcher and helped establish the **Northern Ireland peace process**.

> Roald Dahl (1916–90) :: Born in **Wales** to **Norwegian** parents; served in the RAF. Wrote **Charlie and the Chocolate Factory** and **George's Marvellous Medicine**.

## Labour (1997–2010)
**Tony Blair**'s government created the **Scottish Parliament** and the **Welsh Assembly** (both **1999**). In Northern Ireland the **Good Friday Agreement** was signed in **1998**; the NI Assembly was elected in **1999**, suspended in **2002** and restored in **2007**. **Gordon Brown** became PM in **2007**.

In the **1990s** Britain helped liberate **Kuwait** (after Iraq invaded in **1990**) and acted in the former **Yugoslavia**. Since **2000**, British forces have fought terrorism in **Afghanistan and Iraq**; combat troops left Iraq in **2009**.

## Recent governments
- **2010–2015**: the first **coalition** since 1974 – **Conservatives and Liberal Democrats**, with **David Cameron** as PM.
- **2015–2024**: **Conservative** governments under **Cameron, Theresa May, Boris Johnson, Liz Truss and Rishi Sunak**, including the **2016 EU referendum (Brexit)** and the COVID-19 pandemic.
- **2024**: **Labour**, led by **Keir Starmer**, won a majority at the general election.

@section Great British inventions
Britain gave the world many 20th-century inventions. You **do not need to remember birth and death dates** – focus on **who invented what**.

| Television | **John Logie Baird** (Scottish), 1920s. First TV broadcast London–Glasgow in **1932**.
| Radar | **Sir Robert Watson-Watt** (Scottish). First successful test **1935**.
| Radio telescope | **Sir Bernard Lovell** built it at **Jodrell Bank**, Cheshire – for years the biggest in the world.
| Turing machine | **Alan Turing**, 1930s – a theoretical device that shaped modern computing.
| Insulin | **John MacLeod** (Scottish) was **co-discoverer**; used to treat diabetes.
| DNA structure | Discovered in **1953** at London and Cambridge; **Francis Crick** shared the Nobel Prize.
| Jet engine | **Sir Frank Whittle**, an RAF engineer, **1930s**.
| Hovercraft | **Sir Christopher Cockerell**, **1950s**.
| Concorde | Developed by **Britain and France** – the only supersonic passenger aircraft. First flew **1969**, retired **2003**.
| Harrier jump jet | Designed in the UK – can **take off vertically**.
| ATM (cashpoint) | **James Goodfellow**, 1960s. First used by **Barclays in Enfield, 1967**.
| IVF | **Sir Robert Edwards** and **Patrick Steptoe**. First "test-tube baby" born in **Oldham, 1978**.
| Cloning | **Sir Ian Wilmut** and **Keith Campbell** cloned **Dolly the sheep** in **1996** – the first cloned mammal.
| MRI scanner | **Sir Peter Mansfield** was **co-inventor**.
| World Wide Web | **Sir Tim Berners-Lee**. First used **25 December 1990**.
